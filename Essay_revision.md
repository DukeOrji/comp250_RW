# Introduction
The case of Mexico under the institutional revolutionary party shows how political systems, culture and institutions interact and shape both government and society over time.
My presentation specifically focuses on how the PRI functioned as a "perfect dictatorship," where elections were constantly manipulated to maintain power.
These aspects of my presentation connect with two important topics in the textbook: authoritarian regimes (CH 8) and democratic institutions (CH 10).
There is some evidence that confirms authoritarian regimes manipulate institutions to secure long-term stability, the PRI illustrates this capacity of power.
Those same institutions mold the transition to democracy and continuously influence political outcomes even after their rule ends.

The PRI regime is a strong example of a part-dominant authoritarian system, it can be categorized as a civilian dictatorship as we discussed in chapter 8 (Clark, Golder, and Golder CH. 8).
Distinct from military dictatorships, which is often unstable and are more susceptible to an internal overthrow through a coup de tat.
The PRI managed to maintain its monopoly on power for over 70 years, between 1929 to 2000.
This demonstrates how civilian authoritarian regimes can institutionalize their power.
Although the PRI regularly held elections, these elections were far from fair, as they were manipulated through fraud, repression and voter intimidation.
This reminds me of the idea we discussed that dictatorships are not all the same, some rely more on political structures than direct violence to maintain power.

The problem of authoritarian power sharing, limiting Dictators from ruling alone, since they must rely on appointed elites and party members is evident in the PRI's success, they solved this problem through a system of patronage and corporatism (Clark, Golder, and Golder CH. 8).
The PRI incorporated groups like labor unions and business into the regime and gave them benefits in exchange for loyalty.
This decreases the possibility of internal conflicts or coup de tats since the elites had incentives to support the system rather than oppose it.
Because of this the PRI managed to balance power sharing without losing control.

The problem of authoritarian control, authoritarian regimes often struggle to know what citizens truly think because people hide their true dissatisfactions towards the government until they think enough people are willing to protest.
In Mexico the PRI controlled media and limited criticism, this made it difficult to gauge real public opinion creating unpredictable social outcomes.
In the 1980s and 1990s, public frustration increased, and the regime could no longer utilize the same level of control, this highlights how authoritarian regimes can appear stable but are vulnerable to sudden change.

The PRI could maintain loyalty within their winning coalition which includes party elites and key social groups affiliated with the regime, by distributing private goods, such as jobs, favors and political position.
This ties to the selectorate theory discussed in chapter 8, which explains how leaders stay in power by securing support from a winning coalition (Clark, Golder, and Golder CH. 8).
The PRI's regime was not focused on proving broad public goods but rather keeping its major supporters satisfied.

After viewing this dynamic it's easy to understand how corruption and distrust became so embedded within and around the PRI system. 
Leaders had little to no incentive to provide broad public goods that would benefit the entire population since their winning coalition was relatively small, they focused on maintaining their loyalty.
This reinforces a system where political loyalty is rewarded rather than equality or fairness.
Although this system secured the PRIs stability short term, it also provoked public distrust.
Overtime citizens became increasingly dissatisfied, even if they couldn't express opposition due to the regime's control.
This is a weakness in authoritarian regimes, the same strategy helping them survive can also contribute to the decline.

Chapter 8 explains how the PRI maintained power, while chapter 10 aids in the explanation of what happened during and after the transition to democracy.
The idea that political systems are defined by the relationship between the government, the legislature, and the head of state was derived from chapter 10.
Currently Mexico practices a presidential democratic regime, meaning the president is both the head of state and the head of government and is elected for a fixed term.
Although even after the PRI lost presidency in 2000, it created institutional patterns credible and effective enough to remain in place.

Even though the systems established by the PRI were not fully just/ fair their continuity highlights that institutions do not change overnight.
Overtime, reforms like the creation of the Federal Electoral Institute (now the INE) increased electoral integrity in turn making elections more competitive.
Chapter 10 suggests that elections play a major role in influencing political outcomes and legitimizing systems of authority (Clark, Golder, and Golder CH.10).
So even though the PRI initially used elections to secure a continuous reign, those same institutions eventually played a role in its decline.

This highlights the usefulness and versatility of institutions. 
Under authoritarian rule, elections were used as a tool to secure control over policy while managing to appear legitimate.
However once electoral integrity improved, those same institutions began to act as a mechanism of accountability.
In Mexico's case, as elections became more competitive, the PRI ability to manipulate outcomes decreased.

This reflects how reforms introduced by authoritarian regimes to stabilize themselves can sometimes lead to democratization.
The transition in 2000 can be viewed as a cause of institutional change, the PRI decision to not fully manipulate the election that year aided their loss of power to the PAN.
Once elections became more credible, the PRI could no longer guarantee victory, and power shifted.

However, the PRI legacy had already been well established.
Its clear in Mexico that political behavior was shaped by political institutions, even after democratization, issues like corruption, weak state capacity, and public distrust remained in the government of Mexico.
These issues can be attributed to the era of the PRI when unfair practices like corruption and favoritism in government became acceptable through actions like centralization and patronage.
This highlights the persistence of political culture despite changes in formal institutions.

The PRI built electoral institutions that eventually enabled democratic competition, I believe the conflict between democratic and authoritarian regimes in Mexico suggests that authoritarian systems often establish the foundation for possible democratic systems, while possibly leaving behind lasting oppressive structures/challenges.
This highlights the complexity of political development.
The period between 2012 to 2018 witnessed the rise of the PRI into an influential political position led by Enrique Pena Nieto.
Even in a democratic system, the PRI was able to regain power, showing that political competition had become more open.
Although public distrust and dissatisfaction during their reign reinforced negative assumptions of the entire party.
Although democratic institutions were established, they don't necessarily ensure effective governance.

In general, the case of the PRI reigning over Mexico hihglights and important insight in comparative politics, it's possible that both authoritarian and democratic system are not completely distinct but could exist on a spectrum.
The PRI combines aspects from both regimes, establishing electoral institutions while still maintaining authoritarian control.
This means simply introducing democratic institutions wouldn't automatically guarantee a democratic transition or outcome.
Rather this case shows that institutions can be shaped and manipulated by those in power, in turn making democratic transitions often gradual and incomplete, with elements of the previous regime continuing to influence political behavior and governance.

# Conclusion
To conclude, the PRI reign over Mexico provides us with a strong example of how authoritarian regimes manage authority in a state, and how authoritarian regimes transition into democratic regimes.
I drew concepts and ideas form both chapter 8 and 10 to illustrate how the PRI maintained power through management of its winning coalition, control and patronage, and suggest how institutions like elections and authoritative structures shaped both the political system that followed the transition to democracy in Mexico.
